Firms Already Have an AI Rulebook:
It's Called the UK GDPR

Towo Odukoya · AI & Regulation · October 2026

← Back to Research

I'm often asked about my LLM dissertation in interviews: UK GDPR and AI Regulation in Financial Services, which earned a Distinction at Queen Mary University of London. The short version usually gets a nod. The longer version, which explains why it matters to the firm across the table, usually gets a follow-up question. This post is the longer version.

The Question I Asked

Three-quarters of UK financial services firms already use AI, and another 10% plan to within three years (Bank of England & FCA, 2024). Credit scoring, fraud detection, transaction monitoring, onboarding and customer service all run on personal data.

A common assumption is that AI is a regulatory blank space waiting for a dedicated "AI law". My dissertation tested that. The question I asked was simple: how far does existing UK data protection law already govern AI in financial services, and where does it fall short?

Why It Matters Now

The UK has not followed the EU in passing a single AI Act. Instead, the FCA has said it doesn't plan AI-specific rules. It relies on existing, technology-neutral frameworks such as the Consumer Duty and the Senior Managers & Certification Regime (FCA, 2025). Data protection law carries much of the weight alongside them.

That weight keeps growing. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 rewrote the UK rules on automated decision-making. A decision now counts as "solely automated" if there is no meaningful human involvement (Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, s.80). The ICO has also updated its guidance on AI and data protection, including a new chapter on transparency (ICO, 2026).

For a regulated firm, "we're waiting for AI regulation" is not a strategy. The rules that apply to your models are already on the books.

Three Things Organisations Can Take From It

1. Map every AI use case to a lawful basis and a purpose. AI projects often begin with "let's see what the data can do". Data protection law begins with "why are we processing this, and on what basis?". Firms that ask the second question first move faster later, because they aren't retrofitting compliance onto a live model.

2. Make human review meaningful, not decorative. A person who approves every model output without the time, information or authority to challenge it isn't meaningful oversight. For credit, fraud and onboarding decisions, firms need to show who can override the model, on what basis, and how often they do.

3. Be able to explain the decision to the person it affects. Customers have a right to understand significant decisions made about them. If a firm can't explain in plain English why an application was declined, it has a Consumer Duty problem as well as a data protection one.

Where I've Taken It Since

The research didn't stay on paper. In my current role as an AML Analyst, I designed and delivered AI training that influenced firm policy and our risk appetite. What I kept seeing is that most AI risk comes down to behaviour: what people paste into a tool, what they trust without checking, and what they assume someone else has approved. The law sets the boundaries. Training and culture keep people inside them.

That's why I think this research matters to organisations, not just examiners. Firms that treat data protection as the foundation of their AI governance will be the ones regulators, clients and customers trust to innovate.

Technical Terms & Acronyms

UK GDPR: the UK General Data Protection Regulation, read alongside the Data Protection Act 2018

ICO: Information Commissioner's Office, the UK data protection regulator

FCA: Financial Conduct Authority

SM&CR: Senior Managers & Certification Regime

Automated decision-making: decisions made about people by technology without meaningful human involvement
